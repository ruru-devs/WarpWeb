## **3. What the AI would actually learn**

Your HTTrack idea is a reasonable starting point for investigation, but downloading a website alone isn't sufficient to teach an AI how it works.

You'd need to build a structured representation of the website.

Imagine the engine processes a website and creates the following layers:

## **Website knowledge representation**

**Layer 1 — Content**

Pages, text, images, products, articles, prices, descriptions, FAQs

**Layer 2 — Structure**

Links, categories, page hierarchy, navigation paths, relationships between pages

**Layer 3 — Functionality**

Search, filtering, sorting, forms, buttons, booking, purchasing, downloads

**Layer 4 — Intent mapping**

What users mean, what actions satisfy them, and how to reach the relevant result

This representation becomes the website's **knowledge base**, which your AI consults at inference time.

You may not need to train an LLM at all.

This is a very important distinction.

Instead of training a new model for every website, you could use:

- A general-purpose LLM to understand natural language.
- A crawler to collect website data.
- A parser to extract structure and functionality.
- A database to store the website's knowledge.
- Retrieval to fetch the relevant context.
- A browser automation layer to navigate or execute actions.

This is far cheaper, more maintainable, and easier to update than fine-tuning a separate model for every website.

### **The critical technical distinction: offline knowledge vs live browser state**

A website snapshot can tell you:

- What pages exist.
- Which products are showcased.
- What features are advertised.
- Where certain content is located.
- How the website is structured at crawl time.

But it cannot reliably tell you:

- Whether a restaurant has a reservation available *right now*.
- Whether a product is still in stock.
- Whether the current user's account has access to something.
- What content has appeared since the crawl.
- Whether a button works after authentication.
- What a dynamic interface reveals after interaction.

Therefore, the best architecture is hybrid:

### **Website intelligence + live browser**

**Knowledge base**

Pre-crawled information, website maps, descriptions, features, navigation, and known workflows.

**Live browser inspection**

Current DOM, dynamic data, active tabs, login state, live inventory, interactive components.

**Intent execution**

Answer, navigate, filter, interact, or perform the task with the user's permission.

This also solves the IP-blocking problem you encountered in your original SaaS idea to some extent. Instead of your centralized server constantly crawling and repeatedly accessing websites, much of the live interaction could happen through the user's own browser, from their session and connection. Prebuilt site profiles could be cached and shared, while live requests remain user-driven.

It doesn't eliminate blocks or restrictions, and some sites actively prevent automation, but it changes the dependency significantly.

## **6. The most valuable feature might actually be "show me," not "do it"**

There's a distinction between an AI assistant answering and an AI assistant controlling a website.

I would design three response modes:

**Find**

Find the information and present it in the side panel without navigating away.

**Navigate**

Take the user directly to the relevant page, section, product or control.

**Interact**

Apply filters, fill fields, select options, and perform multi-step operations.

**Complete**

Carry out a complete workflow, with confirmation for sensitive or irreversible actions.

For example, on a university website:

> "Find the form for requesting a transcript."

It shouldn't just reply with a URL. It should open the exact page, or if the form is embedded, bring the form into view.

On an e-commerce website:

> "Find me black running shoes, size 9, under ₹5,000."

It could apply filters, present matching products, and let the user choose.

On GitHub:

> "Show me the commits where the quantum circuit simulator was optimized."

It could search the history, retrieve the relevant commits, and present them with links and summaries.

The less the user has to interpret the AI's answer and manually carry out the remaining steps, the more valuable the product becomes.

## **8. The actual competitive advantage**

The model itself probably won't be your moat. Anyone can call an LLM API.

Your defensibility could instead come from:

- **Website capability maps:** Structured, continually improved understanding of how websites work.
- **Reliable action mappings:** Tested selectors, routes, and workflows that don't break every time a website changes its UI.
- **Intent resolution:** Knowing that different phrases mean the same operation.
- **Community-maintained site profiles:** Users can create, share, and improve profiles for websites.
- **Universal fallback:** Even unsupported sites can be understood dynamically from the current page.
- **Execution reliability:** Actually taking the user where they want to go, rather than merely producing a plausible answer.

The more sites it supports reliably, the more useful the extension becomes.

## **9. The biggest problems you'll eventually face**

I'm enthusiastic about the concept, but these are real engineering hurdles:

- **Dynamic websites:** React, SPA routing, infinite scroll, lazy loading, and content that only appears after interaction.
- **Website changes:** Selectors and workflows can break when websites update their frontend.
- **Crawling limitations:** robots policies, anti-bot measures, access restrictions, rate limiting, and terms of service.
- **Hallucinated actions:** The AI must never invent a button, capability, product, or page.
- **Security:** An agent reading website content is exposed to prompt injection. Website text must be treated as untrusted data, not instructions to the agent.
- **Permission boundaries:** Buying, deleting, submitting, posting, booking, or changing account settings should have appropriate safeguards.
- **Economics:** Crawling, indexing, inference, and browser automation can become expensive if you make every interaction unnecessarily AI-heavy.

These are solvable engineering problems, but reliability is going to matter more than how impressive the AI demo looks.
